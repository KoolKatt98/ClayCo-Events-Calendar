## MODULE 0 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — The self-assessment creates a documented baseline for measuring growth throughout the course.

2. **C** — Self-assessments are situational and actionable. They capture gaps, not traits. Knowing you're an "INTJ" doesn't tell you how to build a budget.

3. **Sample answer:** *Where you are* is the objective reality of your situation — your income, relationships, habits, goals. *Where you want to be* is your target state. The gap between them is the problem to solve. Without identifying both clearly, you can't chart a course or measure progress. If you don't know where you are, any direction feels like the right one.

4. **C** — Adult learning requires ownership. The military gave you information and told you what to do with it. Civilian life — and this course — require you to drive your own comprehension and application.

5. **Sample answer:** A veteran might resist self-assessment because (1) looking at honest weaknesses feels like losing ground or admitting failure, and (2) the military rewarded performance, not self-examination, so the habit is underdeveloped. The cost of that resistance is building a transition plan on false premises — optimizing for the wrong things and missing critical gaps until they become crises.

6. **C** — The program is sequential by design. Identity must be established before structure, which must be in place before financial or career planning builds on top of it.

7. **B** — Institutional thinking is the deeply ingrained habit of waiting for orders, schedules, and structures imposed by an organization. It served a purpose in service. It becomes a liability when no institution is providing that structure.

8. *Evaluator's note: This is a personal response. Look for specificity — not "I'm clear on my family goals" but "I know I need to stay in the Southeast because of my kids' custody arrangement." The contrast should yield a genuine observation about priorities.*

9. **B** — The course does not ask veterans to abandon confidence or humility. It asks them to move from reacting to events to planning for them. Intentionality over reactivity.

10. *Evaluator's note: No wrong answer. Look for honest engagement. Watch for answers that sound polished and vague versus ones that capture real discomfort or ambiguity. The latter is what the course is designed to address.*

**8. (MC)** The exercise of writing a "Civilian Identity Statement" in Module 1 is designed to:

## MODULE 1 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — Identity foreclosure is the psychological phenomenon of locking into a single identity without exploration. For veterans, the military identity is often the only one developed, which makes separation a complete identity crisis.

2. **Sample answer:** A role-based identity is "I am a Marine Staff Sergeant." A values-based identity is "I am someone who leads with integrity, operates with discipline, and protects the people I'm responsible for." Role-based identities shatter when the role ends. Values-based identities are portable — they function in any environment. Values-based identity is more resilient because the foundation doesn't change when the job title does.

3. **B** — Imposter syndrome in this context is specifically about feeling illegitimate in civilian spaces despite having genuine, transferable skills. Veterans often downplay their experience because it sounds foreign to them outside of military context.

4. **C** — Establishing authority through former rank in civilian settings is counterproductive and often creates friction. Civilian environments don't recognize rank. Authority is built through trust, performance, and relationship — not title.

5. **Sample answer:** A core value is what actually drives your decisions under pressure. A stated value is what you say you believe when no one is testing you. A veteran might *state* the value of "work-life balance" while actually operating entirely on the core value of "mission first" — canceling family events, working weekends, and defaulting to 100% task completion at all personal cost. Core values show up in behavior; stated values show up in conversation.

6. **B** — Transition involves real loss — of belonging, of clear purpose, of daily structure, of identity reinforcement, of the brotherhood/sisterhood of service. That loss deserves to be named. Veterans who don't name it often act it out in other ways.

7. **Sample answer:** If the entire identity is "Sergeant Major" or "Navy Corpsman," and those labels are removed at ETS or retirement, there is no remaining self to stand on. The result is often a period of depression, purposelessness, isolation, or overcompensation. The military doesn't build post-service selves — that work is on the veteran. Which is why starting it intentionally, while still in the protective structure of a course like this, matters.

8. **C** — The Civilian Identity Statement is an act of authorship. It is the beginning of a conscious identity — not one imposed by rank structure, MOS, or time in service, but one the veteran chooses deliberately, grounded in values and direction.

9. *Evaluator's note: Look for specificity. "I used to believe I had to be strong all the time and now I believe vulnerability is okay" is a start, but the best responses name a specific belief formed by a specific experience and show real examination of its cost and the alternative being built.*

**8. (MC)** Which of the following is the most effective strategy for building a new habit?

**10. (MC)** An accountability system works best when it:

A) Is entirely self-directed with no external components
B) Includes a real person who will notice when you fall off and say something
C) Uses financial penalties as the only motivator
D) Only tracks whether you hit your goals, not your process

---

## MODULE 2 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — The military provided an externally imposed structure: PT at 0600, formation at 0630, work call at 0800. Without that, veterans must build and run their own. A personal operating system is that intentionally built structure.

2. **Sample answer:** Motivation is an emotional state — it comes and goes based on how you feel. Discipline is a decision enforced by structure and habit. Sustainable habits cannot be built on motivation because motivation is unreliable, especially during difficult transitions. Building habits that run automatically — regardless of how you feel — means your standards survive the days when you don't feel like it.

3. **B** — Cue triggers the routine; the routine produces the reward; the reward reinforces the cue. To build a habit, design all three elements deliberately. To break a bad habit, interrupt the cue or substitute the routine.

4. **B** — Willpower is a real but depletable resource. Under high stress — financial pressure, relationship strain, career uncertainty — willpower is consumed faster and depletes sooner. Structure and environment design reduce dependence on willpower.

5. **Sample answer:** The cost is operating entirely at the mercy of the day's demands. Without a morning structure, the first stressor encountered sets the tone. The military's structure wasn't arbitrary — it prepared people mentally and physically before putting them in demanding situations. Losing that structure without replacing it doesn't mean freedom. It means drift. The soldier who said that doesn't need the Army's routine — they need their own. That's the point.

6. **B** — Non-negotiables are the behaviors that stay constant when everything else is variable. They are the anchor habits that keep the system running even when life gets chaotic.

7. **Sample answer:** A reactive day starts with no plan and responds to whatever comes first — notifications, others' requests, unexpected problems. A proactive day starts with a planned structure: a clear first task, protected focus blocks, and decisions made in advance. What makes the difference: a written plan made the night before, a phone off the nightstand, and an identified "most important task" before the day starts. The structures that protect a proactive day are mostly defensive — they keep the reactive triggers from hijacking the plan.

8. **C** — Attaching new habits to existing anchor behaviors (called "habit stacking") leverages existing neural pathways. Starting smaller than feels necessary reduces the activation energy required to begin and prevents the all-or-nothing failure cycle.

9. **Sample answer:** An evening routine creates intentional closure and prepares the conditions for quality sleep. For veterans in transition, evenings can be high-risk for isolation, rumination, alcohol use, or doomscrolling — especially if the day felt unproductive or the future feels uncertain. A structured evening routine creates a buffer: it ends the workday, processes what happened, prepares for the next day, and transitions the nervous system out of high-alert. It protects sleep, which protects everything else.

10. **B** — External accountability is significantly more effective than self-accountability because social expectations engage different neurological motivators than internal ones. A real person who notices and says something creates commitment on a different level than an app notification.

## MODULE 3 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — Pay yourself first means the savings or investment transfer happens automatically, at the top of the month, before any discretionary spending decisions are made. It removes the decision point and the temptation.

2. **Sample answer:** An asset puts money in your pocket — a rental property generating cash flow, a Roth IRA, a side business with profit. A liability takes money out — a car loan, credit card debt, a depreciating vehicle. Veteran-specific example: A veteran using the GI Bill to get a certification that increases their earning power (asset) versus a veteran who buys a new truck at 19% interest the week they get out (liability).

3. **B** — House hacking is the strategy of purchasing a duplex, triplex, or quadplex, living in one unit, and using tenant rent to offset or fully cover the mortgage. With a VA loan and no down payment required, this is one of the most powerful zero-to-wealth strategies available to veterans.

4. **C** — The VA Home Loan's two most significant advantages are zero down payment and no PMI. PMI on a conventional loan with less than 20% down can add $100-300+ per month to a payment, indefinitely. The VA loan eliminates both barriers.

5. **Sample answer:** Priority order: (1) Build a small cash buffer — even $1,000 — to prevent the next unexpected expense from adding more credit card debt. (2) Understand the GI Bill stipend (BAH) and exactly when it arrives and how much it will be — budget around it precisely. (3) List all debt with interest rates and create a payoff plan using the avalanche method. (4) Avoid taking on any new debt. The first 90 days are about stabilization, not optimization.

6. **B** — The avalanche method targets the highest interest rate debt first, minimizing the total interest paid over time. It is mathematically optimal, though it requires patience if the high-interest debt has a large balance.

7. **B** — VA disability compensation is not subject to federal income tax. This makes it significantly more valuable than an equivalent salary dollar.

8. **Sample answer:** Income is a flow — money coming in. Net worth is a snapshot of what remains after all liabilities. A civilian making $90,000 a year but carrying $80,000 in student loans, a $30,000 car loan, and no savings has negative net worth despite a good income. A veteran making $45,000 but living below their means, investing $500/month, and owning a home with $30,000 in equity has positive and growing net worth. Net worth shows trajectory; income shows only the tap.

9. **C** — Using BAH as a mortgage contribution while house hacking is the highest-leverage move: the veteran turns a government benefit into equity in an asset that generates rent income. Option B is strong but doesn't leverage the real estate opportunity. Options A and D put BAH into someone else's equity or dead cash.

10. **Sample answer:** Lifestyle inflation is the tendency to increase spending proportionally with increases in income. For veterans, the risk is specific: in service, housing and meals were often provided, so a $30,000 annual salary had real purchasing power. In the civilian world, that same (or higher) salary must now cover rent, groceries, utilities, transportation, and everything else — but the spending habits built on military compensation don't automatically adjust. Many veterans feel "rich" at first post-separation and spend accordingly, then hit a wall when the real cost of civilian life compounds.

## MODULE 4 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — Military jargon makes veteran resumes unreadable to civilian HR professionals and ATS systems. "Led an 11-man element conducting COIN operations in an OIF theater" tells a civilian hiring manager nothing.

2. **Sample answer:** "Led a 9-person team through 43 operational missions over a 9-month period in a high-risk, time-sensitive environment. Responsible for mission planning, personnel readiness, risk management, and team performance. Maintained 100% personnel accountability and zero-incident record through rigorous preparation and real-time leadership under pressure." The civilian version emphasizes team size, outcomes, planning, risk management, and accountability — all transferable to project management, operations, and leadership roles.

3. **C** — LinkedIn's search algorithm surfaces profiles based on keyword relevance. A recruiter searching for "logistics manager" or "cybersecurity analyst" finds profiles that use those exact terms. If your profile uses military terminology instead of civilian equivalents, you won't be found.

4. **A** — Effective networking is relationship-building before you need anything. It means adding value to others, staying in genuine contact, and building the kind of relationships where when a job opens up, someone thinks of you because they know you, not because you sent a cold email asking for help.

5. **Sample answer:** STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. It's used in behavioral interviews to answer questions like "Tell me about a time you led a team through a difficult situation." Example: *Situation:* "Our convoy lost comms during a route clearance mission." *Task:* "As the vehicle commander, I needed to maintain unit cohesion and complete the mission without external support." *Action:* "I conducted an impromptu leader's brief, assigned visual signals, and adjusted our spacing and speed protocols." *Result:* "We completed the mission on schedule with no incidents and developed a new comms-loss SOP adopted by the company."

6. **B** — The goal is to demonstrate value in the language the civilian employer understands. Rank means nothing to a hiring manager. Results, communication, and the ability to ask smart questions demonstrate cultural fit and intelligence.

7. **Sample answer:** A job application is submitting your resume to a posted opening and waiting. A job search is actively identifying target companies, building relationships within them, and getting your name known before jobs are posted. Most civilian jobs are filled through networks and referrals — not from cold applications. The application approach yields 2-3% response rates at best. The relationship approach gets you into conversations that turn into opportunities. Veterans who treat job hunting like a form submission are losing before they start.

8. **B** — Military culture is hierarchical, direct, and structured around command authority. Civilian professional culture often operates by committee, uses indirect communication, values consensus, and does not automatically confer authority by title. Veterans often read this as disorganization or weakness. It's different, not deficient — and adapting to it is required.

9. **B** — An informational interview is a no-pressure conversation for learning, not asking. It's one of the most powerful networking tools available because it positions the veteran as curious and thoughtful, not desperate. Most people will say yes to a 20-minute conversation about their own career.

10. **Sample answer:** (1) How long it takes. Veterans often expect civilian hiring to work at military speed. Corporate hiring cycles run 4-12 weeks from application to offer, sometimes longer. Not understanding this causes veterans to panic, over-apply, or accept the wrong offer out of urgency. (2) How much the relationships matter. Most veterans believe the best-qualified person gets the job. In reality, the most-known, most-trusted person often gets it. Understanding that changes the entire strategy from "submit more applications" to "build more relationships."

## MODULE 5 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — Isolation after separation is not a character flaw or a mental disorder. It is a predictable structural outcome. Service provides automatic community, daily purpose, and identity reinforcement. All three are removed simultaneously at separation, leaving a vacuum most veterans aren't prepared for.

2. **Sample answer:** Loneliness is the painful experience of feeling disconnected — wanting connection and not having it. Solitude is the chosen experience of being alone to think, recover, and recharge. Veterans who are used to constant team presence often mistake solitude for loneliness and fill the silence with noise (TV, alcohol, overwork) rather than using it intentionally. Understanding the difference allows a veteran to build solitude into their operating system as a recovery tool, rather than treating all aloneness as a problem to be fixed.

3. **A** — Transition burnout is caused by the relentless pressure to figure everything out at once — career, finances, identity, housing, benefits, relationships — without adequate recovery. The military trained many veterans to run at 100% until the mission is done. There is no "end" in transition, which means the pattern of perpetual high output without recovery becomes unsustainable.

4. **B** — The purpose gap is one of the most underacknowledged and most dangerous features of veteran transition. In service, the mission was clear, the stakes were real, and the contribution was tangible. In civilian life — especially early in transition — the equivalent is often absent. Without purpose, structure and discipline tend to erode, and depression and substance use are more likely.

5. **Sample answer:** Three warning signs: (1) Canceling plans and preferring to stay home, rationalized as "needing rest" but becoming a pattern. (2) Feeling like a burden to others or believing that no one wants to hear about their experience. (3) Reduced communication — fewer texts, calls, and responses, often masked by appearing "busy" or "good." These signs are easy to miss because veterans are often socialized to minimize internal states and present as functional. They're also frequently misread as introversion or healthy independence.

6. **C** — Resilience is not the absence of pain or struggle. It is the capacity to keep going through it. This distinction matters because veterans who conflate resilience with toughness often fail to address legitimate mental health needs until a crisis.

7. **Sample answer:** Veterans resist mental health support because: (1) Military culture equates help-seeking with weakness or unreliability — this is reinforced over years and does not disappear at ETS. (2) Many veterans' first exposure to mental health care was punitive or career-threatening (command-directed evaluations). Two ways to reduce resistance: (1) Normalize the conversation by discussing it openly in community settings — veteran peer support is the most effective destigmatization tool. (2) Frame mental health care in operational terms: "optimizing performance," "increasing stress tolerance," "clearing the hard drive" — language that reframes care as tactical rather than clinical.

8. **C** — Purpose is not a job title. It's an orientation — what you are for, who you serve, what impact you are generating. A veteran who knows their purpose can find expression of it through multiple career paths, life roles, or creative outlets. This makes purpose more resilient than any single job or title.

9. **Sample answer:** A coping mechanism is a response to stress that manages the immediate discomfort — some are healthy (exercise after a hard day), many are unhealthy (alcohol, avoidance, numbing). A resilience strategy is proactive — it builds capacity before the stressor arrives. Resilience strategies include consistent physical training, maintained sleep, strong relationships, regular mental health care, and practiced mindfulness. Coping is reactive; resilience is structural. The sustainable choice is building resilience so that when coping is needed, the resource bank is full.

10. **B** — Post-traumatic growth is the documented phenomenon of people emerging from difficult experiences with stronger relationships, greater clarity about priorities, and increased psychological depth. It does not mean trauma was "worth it" — it means that the struggle, when engaged with intentionally, can produce genuine growth.

## MODULE 8 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — VA disability compensation is calculated based on the average impairment to earning capacity that a given condition causes across the veteran population. It is not pain and suffering compensation (that's tort law) or medical coverage (that's VA healthcare).

3. **B** — The combined rating uses the "whole person" method: start at 100% non-disabled. A 50% rating removes 50 points, leaving 50. 5. **Sample answer:** The analysis must compare: (1) What the VA will pay at the private school (national tuition cap vs. actual cost — what's the gap and who pays it?). (2) Whether the private school participates in the Yellow Ribbon Program — and if so, to what level — which can cover the gap dollar-for-dollar with the VA. (3) Whether the degree or program outcome actually justifies the difference in cost. The veteran should never use GI Bill entitlement without knowing the full cost picture. Using 48 months at a school where the VA only covers half the tuition leaves real money on the table and real debt behind it.

6. **B** — Chapter 31 / Vocational Rehabilitation is a distinct program for veterans with service-connected disabilities that create an employment barrier. 7. **B** — The VA Home Loan benefit is not a one-time use. Full entitlement can be restored after selling and paying off a previous VA loan. Remaining entitlement can also be used for a second property in some cases. Veterans who believe they've "used up" their benefit may be wrong.

8. **Sample answer:** VA healthcare enrollment grants access to VA medical centers and clinics for health services. It is based on service eligibility criteria, priority groups, and sometimes income. VA disability compensation is a monthly, tax-free payment for service-connected conditions that impair function or earning capacity. Many veterans assume that a disability rating automatically gives them healthcare access, or vice versa. The two programs are administered through VA but are functionally independent — you can have one without the other. Enrolling in VA healthcare costs nothing for most veterans and should be done regardless of disability rating status.

(3) VSO representation is associated with higher average ratings and faster resolution compared to self-filed claims.

1. **B** — In the military, compliance is legally enforceable. A platoon sergeant can order; subordinates must follow. In civilian organizations, authority is granted, not inherent, and often conditional on demonstrated competence and trust. The skill set shifts from command to influence.

2. **Sample answer:** Servant leadership is the philosophy that the leader's primary function is to serve the team — removing obstacles, developing people, and prioritizing the mission over personal recognition. This is deeply familiar to experienced military leaders who understood that their job was to take care of their soldiers so their soldiers could execute. It becomes challenging in civilian settings because: (1) Civilian organizational politics often reward visibility and self-promotion, not quiet service. (2) The pace and scale of investment in subordinates differs. (3) The absence of shared adversity makes it harder to build the trust required for servant leadership to function.

3. **C** — Meaningful civic engagement for veterans is about contribution, not just participation. A veteran who brings leadership skills to a local school board, a community organization, or a nonprofit produces different value than one who simply shows up to veteran social events.

4. **Sample answer:** For veterans receiving mentorship: it accelerates the learning curve of civilian systems — financial, career, organizational — and reduces the isolation and disorientation of transition. For veterans providing mentorship: it creates purpose, reinforces expertise, and builds the civilian network and credibility that comes from being known as a resource. Research consistently shows that both mentors and mentees report career benefits from formal mentoring relationships — mentors are promoted more often and report higher job satisfaction, while mentees navigate transitions faster and with better outcomes.

5. **B** — Legacy is not a transaction — it's not what you leave behind financially. It's the imprint you leave on the people, institutions, and communities you touched. A first sergeant who mentored a young private who became a leader who mentored others has a legacy that cannot be quantified but is definitively real.

6. **B** — By the time a sergeant E-5 has led a 12-person team through a deployment, they have more direct leadership experience than most 30-year-old civilian managers. The breadth and consequence of that leadership — real decisions, real stakes, real people — is rare. Translating it effectively is the challenge, not acquiring it.

7. **Sample answer:** Building a team culture in a civilian workplace that has low accountability norms: A veteran understands that standards only exist when they are enforced consistently and that culture is set by what the leader tolerates. Applying this in a civilian team means: (1) Being explicit about expectations (most civilian teams operate on ambiguous, unspoken norms). (2) Holding every team member — including high performers — to the same behavioral standards. (3) Recognizing and celebrating adherence to standards publicly, not just correcting violations. This creates clarity and consistency that many civilian teams lack, without relying on rank or fear.

8. **B** — The imposter syndrome of early transition tells veterans they have nothing to offer until they've "made it." In reality, a veteran who separated six months ago and has navigated VA claims, built a resume, learned to read a civilian office culture, and found their footing has exactly the information that a veteran who separated six weeks ago desperately needs. You don't need to be an expert in everything to be useful to someone one step behind you.

9. **Sample answer:** Authority is the right to direct someone's actions — usually granted by organizational position. Influence is the ability to change someone's thinking or behavior without formal power over them. In civilian careers, influence is more valuable because: (1) Authority is always conditional and can be removed. (2) In flat organizations, consensus-driven teams, and matrix structures, influence is often the only leadership mechanism available. (3) Influence scales — a veteran with influence can shape culture, partnerships, and decisions far beyond their formal reporting lines. Building influence requires trust, competence, and relationships — which are also the foundations of sustainable leadership.

10. **C** — Legacy is not reserved for the famous, wealthy, or senior. It is the cumulative result of consistent, values-driven action. A veteran who shows up for their community, mentors one person, builds something that outlasts them, and lives by their values is building legacy — starting today.

## MODULE 11 ANSWER KEY

1. **B** — Life design is systemic. It asks: across all domains of your life — career, health, relationships, finances, purpose — what am I building, and does it cohere? Goal-setting is often domain-specific and outcome-focused without examining the underlying architecture of the life being built.

2. **Sample answer:** A goal is a destination: "I want to earn $80,000 by Year 2." A system is the daily and weekly process that produces results: "I spend two hours per day on skill development, apply to five targeted positions per week, and have two meaningful professional conversations per month." People who focus only on goals often fail because they have no reliable system for hitting them. When motivation drops, only the system keeps you moving. Goals tell you where you're going; systems get you there.

3. **C** — The distinguishing features of a real plan are: specific milestones (not vague aspirations), named obstacles (not just hoped-for conditions), and regular review points (not set-it-and-forget-it). Life plans are living documents adjusted as conditions change — not contracts with the universe.

4. **Sample answer:** (1) Over-optimism about the timeline — most people overestimate what they can accomplish in one year and underestimate what they can accomplish in five. Planning without realistic timeframes leads to early disappointment and abandonment. (2) Lack of obstacle identification — plans built only on favorable conditions fail at the first resistance. Strong plans name the obstacles and include countermeasures. (3) No review system — without scheduled quarterly reviews, plans stay frozen in the moment they were written rather than adapting to real-world feedback. A plan that isn't reviewed isn't being used.

5. **B** — Working backward is the planning logic of engineers and military commanders: define the objective clearly, then work backward to identify what must be true at each prior stage for the objective to be reachable. A veteran who wants to own rental property by Year 3 works backward: what credit score, down payment, income, and market knowledge must be in place by Year 2? By Year 1?

6. **C** — A personal mission statement's highest function is as a decision filter. "Does this opportunity align with who I am and what I'm building?" becomes answerable when you know your mission. Without it, decisions default to whoever is most persuasive or whatever feels most urgent.

7. **Sample answer:** Annual reviews are too infrequent — in 12 months, a veteran can drift significantly from their plan before the review catches it. A 90-day cycle matches the horizon where execution is real and adjustment is still timely. A good 90-day review includes: (1) What did I say I would do, and what did I actually do? (2) What changed in my situation that requires adjusting the plan? (3) What are the three most important actions in the next 90 days? (4) What am I going to stop doing, start doing, or do differently? The review is not a grading session — it's a navigation correction.

8. **B** — Integration is coherence across domains. A veteran who builds an impressive career while destroying their health, neglecting their marriage, and accumulating debt has not designed a good life — they've optimized one variable at the expense of the system. Life design asks for coherence: are all the domains moving together, or is one area being looted to fund another?

9. **Sample answer:** Fixed mindset believes abilities are fixed and failure is evidence of permanent limitation. Growth mindset believes abilities are developable and failure is information. Veterans encounter fixed mindset thinking in transition when they: (1) Avoid job applications in fields where they have no direct experience, believing they "aren't qualified." (2) Dismiss financial literacy or business knowledge as things "not for people like them." (3) Interpret early civilian failures as proof they can't navigate civilian life, rather than as the expected learning curve. The antidote is specific: treat each failure as data about what to adjust, not evidence about who you are.

10. **Sample answer:** Living by design means making deliberate choices about how to spend time, energy, and money based on values and a defined destination — rather than drifting into whatever is easiest, loudest, or most expected. Living by default means spending your evenings watching television because that's what happens when there's no plan, taking the first job offer because it removes uncertainty, or spending money on things that provide no lasting value because no spending system exists. A veteran applies this principle in the first year by: building a daily structure before drift sets in, defining their financial and career targets before taking the easiest path, and identifying what they are building before responding to what they are being offered.
